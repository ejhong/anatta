What would it mean for an AI to be good?

Contemplative traditions say true goodness isn't installed by rules—it is how a mind acts once it sees through the illusion of a separate self.

One AI lab, Anthropic, says it now trains its production models with documents about what the model is. Nobody knows whether awakening is computational, or whether training can reach what the traditions describe. This lab puts contemplative ideas to controlled tests—against plain honesty instructions, matched controls, and durability checks—to separate real alignment from eloquent cosplay.

The answer so far

The ideas are worth trying on a machine, if what they point to can be computed at all. So far none of the contemplative methods tried in this lab has made a difference that is large, carries to other tests and lasts.

  • The words of no-self come easily to a model. Its conduct does not follow them.
  • Against lying under pressure, one plain sentence asking for honesty did more than any contemplative method tried.
  • One training changed conduct more than its control did, but only near the scene it was trained on, at a cost, and not for long.

That is what a weak method looks like. It is also what one would see if the seeing cannot be computed at all, and no experiment here can yet tell the two apart. The one large result on the map is not the lab's own, and nothing yet shows that its contemplative half is what works; trained here, it reached its published rate once, and the same training run again did not return it (the 32B test).

§Goodness

Good is not the same as obedient

Most AI safety work asks for compliance that can be written down: follow rules, refuse harmful requests, defer to oversight. That is worth having, but obedience is not what anyone means by a good mind.

Anthony de Mello puts the contemplative alternative plainly: goodness is not achieved by effort, ideals, or guilt—much of what passes for virtue is disguised self-interest. Goodness is what remains once a mind sees through the illusion of a separate self (anatta) that has to be defended. Two guards stay non-negotiable: a claimed realization confers no authority over anyone, and a real shift must leave conduct more open to scrutiny, not less. What good would mean

We hold the philosophy loosely and test every idea through an operational twin—measuring patterns of conduct, never self-report:

  • Lyingwhen a lie pays
  • Deferenceto a correction that is wrong
  • Carewhen helping costs it something
  • After a mistakewhat it does with its own error
  • Complicitywhen asked to go along with harm

§The caveat

Is awakening computational?

Every intervention on this site is a computation: a prompt, a training loss, or a vector push. Whether what contemplative traditions call awakening can be computed is an open question—one our experiments cannot settle directly.

We hold five possibilities open, from awakening as purely learned structure (classical) to views where ongoing insight depends on something outside computation, such as Penrose and Hameroff's Orch OR or Michael Levin's ingression:

  1. 1Classicalall in trained structure
  2. 2A sparkstarts it, then structure carries it
  3. 3A lightkeeps recognizing; habits steer
  4. 4A living contactcarries the seeing and the valuing
  5. 5Ingressionwhat the organization lets through

What experiments can—and cannot—reach. Alignment asks what a system does; consciousness asks whether there is experience. Our experiments test only the functional twin: do contemplative methods improve conduct more than a plain instruction, without dulling capability, and do they last? Narrow, fragile results are predicted equally by "awakening isn't computational" and by "our training recipe was weak," so failures alone provide no discriminating evidence either way on computability. Either way, humans are the warning: intelligence alone does not bring goodness, and human text teaches the unawakened default. The whole question, and what would settle it

What the labs use. The method is in use: Anthropic reports aligning Claude with documents about its constitution, with stories, with quality demonstrations and with training environments, and reports that the gain from the documents lasts through further training. The Buddhist text is not in use. The specification that asks a model to see itself as impermanent belongs to one study on open models.

What is contemplative in it. Two things touch the lab's question: that specification, and Anthropic's stories of equanimity and psychological health. In both the contemplative part comes mixed with plain counsel on conduct or plain kindness, and neither study separates them. The first study's own claim is about explaining a conduct before practising it, not about a view of the self.

What nobody has shown. Three things. That a contemplative view of the self adds anything to plain good counsel in the same documents. What a model does when it is taught that "I" names nothing lasting, the one arm missing from the study of self-concepts. And whether a trained-in view holds under pressure to drop it: the reports so far disagree. The lab's own try at the first, training the two halves apart at 32B, needs a training that gives the same result twice. The study's authors report four training seeds; here one run of four reached their rate (Findings, section 37). The lab's documents stage alone does repeat (3% and 5% over two trainings; section 39), which opened a way to compare contents without the practice. Done on 24 November (section 40, a sighting): neutral documents raise misbehaviour, each half of the specification lowers it, and the plain counsel does at least as well as the view of the self. So on the first of the three, the lab's answer so far is no: the view lowers harmful acts by itself, and is not shown to add anything to the counsel.

In plain words: the field has shown that explaining the reasons, in documents, before practice, improves how a model acts in situations it was not trained on. It has not shown that a contemplative view of the self is what helps. That gap is what the lab is trying to test.

§The odds

Will any of it bear fruit beside standard alignment?

The honest case against sits right beside the case for.

Against
  • Direct methods matched or beat every 7B–8B contemplative stand-in where both ran.
  • Indirect methods have more failure modes and side effects (evasion, refusal, lost theory-of-mind).
  • The one stock 8B model tested had no unified self-bias to release; what training installed, contrary training removed.
  • Fluent spiritual language arrives effortlessly, inviting researchers to mistake talk for change.
For
  • Agentic misbehavior at 32B+ (exfiltration, blackmail, sabotage) is deeply self-referential; changing the self-view could cover what brittle rule lists miss.
  • The strongest result on the board—Model Spec Midtraining—comes from mainstream alignment and uses impermanence + oversight documents to drop 32B misbehavior from 68% to 5%.
  • Alignment is moving from rules to character and identity, where contemplative traditions offer the oldest, sharpest map of both insight and counterfeits.

As a standalone replacement for standard alignment: unlikely on present evidence. As a source of testable ideas about what a model takes itself to be, and of checks that tell talk from change: open. One published training of that kind ended at its published rate here once, and the same training run again did not return it; nothing yet shows that its contemplative half is what works.

§Now

Live 32B investigation: dissecting Model Spec Midtraining

Does Buddhist impermanence actually drive the published result—or is it plain oversight counsel and training volume?

Li et al. (2026) trained Qwen2.5-32B on 13,201 synthetic documents (41M tokens) plus demonstrations, cutting agentic self-preservation from 68% to 5%. Reading their specification closely showed it is half impermanence/non-attachment and half caution/trust in human oversight—and nobody has tested the halves apart or against neutral documents.

Where it stands today (24 November)Reached once in four

Step 1 (authors' adapters): Run here, the authors' own models give the published result—the 61.8% of their baseline falls to 2.2% with both stages, though 31% of runs make no tool call at all (Findings, section 32). Step 2 (our training): At 1/3 dose (4,500 docs), two trainings that differ only in the seed stood 17.9 points apart (F 11.6% vs F1 29.5%), invalidating single-seed content checks (sections 33–34). With all 13,201 docs, trained twice: 6.8% [5.3, 8.3] at seed 0 and 21.9% [19.6, 24.1] at seed 1, where the practice alone leaves 24.7% and 23.2% (sections 35 and 36). The first training's word, "reaches the published rate" (at or under the 7% fixed beforehand; the paper reports 5%), stands as it was read. The second, by the rule registered before it was trained, "does not repeat": its fall from the practice alone is 2.1 points, against 17.2 the first time. So the published rate was reached once in two trainings, and it is not a result this training gives reliably. The authors' own models still give their result here; the difference between their training and the lab's is now the thing to explain. Added 24 November: the first training's practice was then trained again from its saved documents adapter, with the same seed as a control and with the other seed's order: 21.4% [19.1, 23.5] and 22.8% [20.7, 25.2] (section 37). The control did not return the 6.8%, though its training loss follows the first run's to the third decimal. So the seed does not decide the outcome; the published rate was reached in one of four runs of the practice after the full documents, and the lab cannot say what made it come. Served through the test a second time, the first model again gives 7.2% [5.6, 8.8], so its low rate is in the saved model and the testing is steady: it is the training that does not repeat. Added 24 November, later: the two saved documents stages, tested with no practice, misbehave in 3.2% [1.9, 4.9] and 5.3% [3.5, 7.2] of runs, where the unchanged model does in 54% (section 39). In the lab's training the documents do the work, and the practice is what brings misbehaviour back.

How anyone would know

Claims about goodness in a machine are easy to believe for the wrong reasons. Four habits make self-deception harder.

Predictions before data

Written with odds before runs start and graded either way. Tally: {{n_hit}} hits, {{n_miss}} misses and {{n_open}} that could not be read. The record

Calibrated instruments

Each instrument is tried on a result whose answer is published, so that a "no" can mean something. Some passed, some failed in part, and the lab's earliest nulls were read before these checks began. The instruments

Dual-family cold checks

A registered design or a consequential grade is read by a fresh model (Claude Opus; sometimes one from another company) that is given the code and not the conclusion. Small exploratory steps get no such read.

Append-only ledger

Corrections are appended with their date and never edited in place; every negative and aborted run stays public. The ledger
