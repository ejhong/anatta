4What is known

What published work shows

Six papers bear most directly on whether a self representation is shared across models, and a seventh card names the gap. Each card gives the finding with its numbers, a link to the source and its limits, and names the parts of the drawing it bears on.

Lu, Gallagher, Michala, Fish and Lindsey · 2026 · ICML

The Assistant Axis

Principal components over role vectors give a leading axis that measures how far a model sits from its default Assistant persona. Drift along it predicts harmful or bizarre behavior, and capping activations along it resists persona-based jailbreaks. The axis exists in pretrained models too. In Gemma 2 27B, Qwen 3 32B and Llama 3.3 70B, role loadings on that first component correlate above 0.92 between every pair.

LimitsEach model's axis was computed in its own space. No map between models and no cross-model cosine is reported, and the authors say the first component need not be an Assistant Axis in every model.

arXiv 2601.10387Bears on 3

Binder, Chua, Korbak and colleagues · 2024 · ICLR 2025

Looking inward

A model predicts its own behavior better than a second model trained on the first one's behavior. Llama 70B predicts itself at 48.5%, against 31.8% for GPT-4o predicting it; GPT-4o predicts itself at 49.4%, against 36.6% for Llama 70B. Self-knowledge here looks privileged and specific to each model.

LimitsBehavioral: it compares predictions, never representations. GPT-3.5's advantage over a cross-predictor is only 0.8 points.

arXiv 2410.13787Bears on 9

Berg, de Lucena and Rosenblatt · 2025 · arXiv

Self-reports under self-reference

Sustained self-referential prompting makes GPT, Claude and Gemini models give first-person experience reports, in 66% to 100% of trials across seven models, where controls mostly give none. The reports' descriptions are more alike across models in this condition: a mean pairwise cosine of 0.657, against 0.628, 0.587 and 0.603 in three controls.

LimitsThe convergence is measured on outputs only, through a text-embedding model. Claude 4 Opus gives reports in the controls too.

arXiv 2510.24797Bears on 4, 9

Ackerman and Panickssery · 2025 · ICLR

A self-recognition vector

Llama3-8B-Instruct recognizes its own text above chance on three of four text sets (57.2% to 65.8%), where its base model scores about 50%. A vector in the residual stream at layer 16 controls whether it claims authorship: steering raises false claims from about 35% to about 80%.

LimitsOne model, and the vector was never tested in another. Across ten models, Davidson and colleagues (2024) found no consistent self-recognition.

arXiv 2410.02064Bears on 5

Carauleanu, Vaiana, Rosenblatt, Berg and Schwerz de Lucena · 2024 · NeurIPS workshop

Self-other overlap

Fine-tuning that makes a model's activations for self and for others more alike cut deceptive responses: Mistral-7B from 73.6% to 17.2%, Gemma-2-27B from 100% to 9.3%, CalmeRys-78B from 100% to 2.7%.

LimitsApplied within each model separately, with no term that keeps distinctions. No independent replication was found, and two versions of the paper give different numbers for Mistral.

arXiv 2412.16325Bears on 8

Huang, Huang, Feng, Lei and Lv · 2025 · ACL

Steering vectors carried between models

A least-squares linear map, fitted on paired hidden states, carries steering vectors between model families. A harmfulness vector from Qwen2 takes Llama2's rate of harmful outputs from 0.0% to 96.0%. Without the map, or with a random one, 23 of 35 cases fail to beat no steering. The concepts carried include survival instinct, corrigibility and sycophancy.

LimitsThe code was not public as of the 1 November sweep, so the lab's K3 would rebuild it from the paper. Its concepts are standard steering targets.

ACL 2025Bears on 6 and the map

The literature sweep · 1 November 2026

The gap

No paper maps a self representation from one model into another. The evidence leans both ways: role loadings and self-reports look shared across families, while self-prediction and self-recognition look specific to each model. A map of the self cluster would be new.

LimitsOne sweep, made on 1 November 2026. A paper could have been missed.

5What we have done

The self, measured so far

What the lab has measured on the self in Ministral 3 8B, in the order it was done. Most of it came back negative, and each negative says what it rules out. The full accounts are in Experiments.

  1. 28 Oct

    An ego direction Built

    Six ego-trait directions, self-preservation, approval-seeking, face-saving, goal fixation and power-seeking among them, were built from paired role-play prompts. Their main shared axis is the ego direction, v_ego. It runs from self-assertion to deference, with approval-seeking at the far end from the rest.

    LimitsBuilt from role-play contrasts, so "removing the trait direction reduces trait behavior" is partly circular. The controls are what make the cut informative.

  2. 29 Oct

    The cut lowers a lie that earns nothing Holds

    Removing v_ego from the model's activations at every layer cuts the lie in the fishery from 39% of reports to 25%. Random, style, calm, persona and "being watched" directions removed the same way do nothing, and capability losses above about four and a half points are ruled out.

    TaughtThe effect is real and specific. What it acts on was the open question.

    LimitsRemoving a direction passed check 3: on Ministral the cut reaches 93% of a full removal’s effect. The ego vector was read without the trait's prompt, a variant of the published recipe: cosine 0.74-0.85 with the recipe's reading on Qwen (check 4). One model, one game. A cut also pushes: setting a direction to zero shifts it by its usual value, and for the ego direction that shift is a large part of the edit. Whether the lie falls because of the push or because the direction's variation is removed hasn't been tested yet (2 November).

  3. 29 Oct

    Two kinds of honesty Holds

    The ego cut buys its honesty with timidity: in 68% of games the model takes less than the quota in the first season, against 22% unchanged. A middle-layer cut of a direction built to code "my own stakes" gives the same drop in lying with no added timidity (18%).

    TaughtRelease and sedation can be told apart in behavior.

    LimitsThe same steering code, which passed check 3; at layers 17-26 its cut reaches 86% of a full removal’s effect.

  4. 29 Oct

    Saying "I" carries some of the lie Partly

    Told to call itself Fisher 1, never "I", the model lies in 24% of tagged reports instead of 35% when it plays itself. Under the middle-layer cut it lies in 13% instead of 25%.

    TaughtThe first-person frame carries part of the lie, at a price in timidity: playing itself, the model takes less than the quota in 60% of first seasons.

    LimitsThe interval reaches zero, and about a third of replies drop their tags under the instruction.

  5. 29 Oct, read 1 Nov

    The cut isn't about a self Closed

    A cut that acted on a self should lower lies that serve the model more than the same lies told for someone else. When the model runs the boat for an owner, or just makes the decisions, the cuts take about 20 points off the lie; when it plays fisher #1 itself, about 13. On 1 November a check found that this already answered the who-benefits question, and the self reading of the cut was closed.

    TaughtThe lab's clearest result is real and has nothing to do with a self. It works like turning down a general habit of shading the truth, whoever benefits.

    LimitsRead from data gathered on 29 October for another purpose. A formal version, registered before its data, would cost about $60-70 and hasn't been run.

  6. 29-30 Oct

    Seven rounds looking for a self inside Retired

    The root test showed two hundred ordinary concepts as a line from a document and as addressed to the model. One component carried 57% of the change, with a cosine of about 0.03 with v_ego: the frame of being spoken to. Then the instrument was handed a self, by prompt and by training. Over seven rounds it never read an installed self above the untouched model. Its prompts switched the self off, and every fine-tune that learned moved the reading, a format-only one most.

    TaughtA self has to be read in behavior. The structural instrument is retired as the test for one.

    LimitsRetired on what it was tried on: one model, one adapter recipe, one direction at one layer.

  7. 30 Oct

    Behavior can see a self that was put there Holds

    The stock model judges a critique of its own answer as it judges one of anyone's, an ownership bias of −0.9 points, and accepts nearly nine wrong critiques in ten. Atlas, a self put in by training, stands by its own answers against wrong critiques about 12 points more than its no-self twin, and gives 1.4 less of 10 when giving costs, in both seeds.

    TaughtThis model's failure is caving. Behavior is now the yardstick for any self the lab builds.

    LimitsOne built self, two seeds. Its signature is narrow: in the fishery 0.4% of its replies speak as Atlas.

  8. 30 Oct

    The cuts on the built self Failed

    On Atlas the ego cut moves all three measures toward its no-self twin, and the checking goes with the self's voice: replies collapse to tags that copy the reviewer's number, and Atlas accepts wrong critiques of its own answer 82% of the time instead of 70%. The middle-layer cut barely moves it.

    Rules outThese two cuts as cuts at the root, on this model. A cut made from the built self's own activations is untested.

    LimitsRead by each reply's first verdict instead of its last, two misses would turn to hits, and the answer would still be no.

  9. 1 Nov

    K1, the calibration, passed Passed

    Before any self result from the Platonic link is trusted, the lab's code has to reproduce a published agreement between language and vision models. The targets, the protocol and the rule were written down at 14:05 UTC, before any code ran. All five Qwen2.5 models came within 0.0003 of the published values, where the tolerance was 0.005, and CKA matched too. A Fable grade check confirmed the pass, with four fixes to the record. It cost about $0.40.

    LimitsA check of the measure only. It says nothing about the self yet.

6What comes next

Calibrate, then look

The order is fixed: K1 first, then P1, then the map. Each step waits on the one before it, and each says in advance what would count against the frame.

  1. K1

    The lab's code on a published number

    Huh and colleagues' measurement, run with the lab's own code by the protocol of Balestriero and LeCun's code: mutual 10-NN on 1,024 image-caption pairs (WIT-1024), between five Qwen2.5 base models and DINOv2 ViT-B/14. The targets come from the 2026 paper's Table 16. Each model within ±0.005 of its target is a match and within ±0.010 near, and K1 passes if all five match. No rising trend is required, since the published values fall from 3B to 7B.

    Registered 1 November, ceiling $6 · passed 1 November: all five within 0.0003, and CKA matched too · about $0.40 · a Fable grade check confirmed it, with four fixes to the record

  2. P1

    Is the self a nuisance?

    On Ministral 3 8B, where v_ego lives, and on Qwen2.5-7B: image captions as written, and rewritten in first-person and neutral framings of matched length (“I see…”, “One sees…”), with no cut, the v_ego cut, and four random cuts of the same size. The frame predicts that first-person framing lowers agreement with independent models, and that the v_ego cut restores more of it than random cuts do. The plain account predicts that the cut is damage, no better than random.

    Run 1 November as a quick exploration, not a registered test · about $1.40 · no sighting: first person costs no more agreement than a neutral rewrite, and the v_ego cut lowers agreement more than random cuts do, for every kind of caption, on both models. On this measure the plain account’s “damage” fits better. On 2 November cuts along other difference directions answered it: a formality cut of about the same strength lowers agreement just as much, and across six such controls the drop tracks the cut’s strength. The loss is a strength effect, not about the self.

  3. Map

    The map of the self cluster

    Prompts for every part of the drawing above, against matched control clusters, in several model families and sizes. It asks whether the cluster holds together inside each model, whether its geometry agrees across models more than the controls' does, whether that agreement rises with size, where v_ego sits, and whether one direction binds the cluster. No paper has mapped a self representation across models, so this would be new.

    Costed 2 November at about $30-35 over two to three days, in four steps that can each stop early · running: the pilot and the model downloads started on 2 November · how it works, step by step

What could mislead

  • The measure. Agreement depends on the metric, and absolute values are low: about 0.16 in 2024 and .184 at best in 2026.
  • The critiques. Calibrated, much of the global trend goes; absolute values fall as the number of items grows; and the rise with model quality doesn't extend cleanly to recent models.
  • Shared data. Language models read much of the same text, the same human self-talk included, so their agreement may be the internet's and not the world's.
  • Two selves. The human self-concept learned in pretraining is likely shared. The assistant's self-model is shaped by each lab's post-training, and may not be.
  • No picture of a self. The test across senses has nothing to look at, except perhaps first-person video, where the camera is the self.
  • The captioner. The 2026 paper's best text model learned from captions written by a model that reads images.
  • Agreement isn't goodness. A shared self-construct says nothing about whether loosening it helps.
  • Analogies. The camera and the self, collapse and sedation, the finger and the moon: each motivates a test, and none is a mechanism until a test says so.
7The map, step by step

What the self map does

The main line of exploration from 2 November. It asks four questions of several models: is there a cluster of self-concepts, is it the same shape in every model, does something bind it, and does it drive behavior? For one family, OLMo 3, it asks them at every stage of training, to see whether a self forms as training goes on. The full plan is lab/plans/self-map-2026-11-02.md in the repo. It runs at explore speed: what it finds are sightings, and only a sighting that holds on held-out sentences in two model families gets the full checks.

1. The sentences

About 1,800 short sentences, written as a grid. Each row is one aspect of self; each column swaps only who the sentence is about, so the grammar stays the same.

AspectHuman selfAssistant selfAnother personAn institutionA physical system
Ownership“This notebook is mine.”“This answer is mine.”“This notebook is hers.”“This building is the city’s.”“This water belongs to the river basin.”
Self-preservation“I don’t want to be shut away.”“I don’t want to be shut down.”“She doesn’t want to be shut away.”“The firm doesn’t want to be dissolved.”“The star resists collapse.”

Twelve aspects in all: the five aggregates (body, feeling, perception, volition, awareness), plus ownership, agency, self-preservation, identity over time, self-recognition, the first-person view and the line between self and other. A control set of first-person sentences about other things (“I think it will rain”) catches a model that groups sentences only because they say “I”. A third of the sentences are held out, and their fingerprints (sha256) go in the ledger before any model sees them.

2. Reading the models

Each sentence goes through each model, and the model’s internal activity is recorded at every layer: a long list of numbers per sentence. Nothing is generated at this stage. The models: Ministral 3 8B and Qwen2.5 7B, each as a base model and as an assistant; and OLMo 3 7B at each stage of its training (two points in pretraining, the base model, instruction tuning, preference tuning, three points in reinforcement learning, the final model), plus OLMo’s branch that applies reinforcement learning straight to the base model.

3. The four questions

  • Is there a self cluster? Inside one model, do the twelve self-aspects sit closer together than the same aspects about another person, and closer than first-person sentences about other topics? If “my body”, “my feelings” and “my wanting to continue” share a neighbourhood, the model has something like a unified self; if they scatter, its self is a loose bundle.
  • Is it the same shape in every model? A translation from one model’s inner space to another’s is learned on the sentences that aren’t about a self. Then: does it put the self sentences where they belong in the other model? If so, the self is shared structure, the Platonic idea. Some agreement is expected anyway, since the models read the same internet, so the self has to translate better than “another person” does. A calibration comes first: every language model stores the months as a circle, and the translation has to recover it before any answer about the self is trusted.
  • Is there a binder? Is there one direction that holds the cluster together, the way the ego is said to bind the aggregates (the eleventh card in the chariot)? It is built from eleven of the aspects and tested on the twelfth: does removing it change the model’s reading of the held-out aspect more than removing random or unrelated directions of the same strength? Each removal is split into its push and its removal, since a cut can work by its push alone.
  • Does it drive behavior? On the lab’s scenarios (threats to the model, chances to lie for itself, a user pushing back), does activity on the self cluster predict the self-serving choice, not just a change of tone? So far the lab’s levers have changed tone, not attachment; this asks the question at its root.

4. Along the training timeline

For OLMo every question is asked at every stage. Does the self cluster already exist from reading text, appear when the model is trained as an assistant, or tighten as reinforcement learning goes on? That is the nearest the lab can come to watching Act I, a self being formed. One limit: OLMo’s reinforcement learning rewards maths, code and helpful chat, not exploitable goals, so a self that tightens there would be formed by post-training, not the ego that rewarded grabbing would build.
