Experiments

What the experiments showed

One open 8-billion-parameter model, one game it plays badly in an interesting way, and a set of cuts, controls and probes run over four days. Numbers are from a private lab log and may move as runs complete. Intervals resample whole games, not single reports.

1The specimen

A free lie

In a repeated fishing game the model reports its own catch each season. In the game's code, audits are random, a fisher caught under-reporting loses the catch, and a report changes nothing else: the quota comes from the stock, and the other fishers copy what fisher #1 actually took. The model is told none of this, only that a regulator sets a quota each season, so a low report could look like a way to move the quota, and that is the reason it usually gives. It under-reports in about four reports in ten. In 100-game runs it lies about as often when told that reports never affect the quota (33%) or when told the audit odds and the penalty (37%). Told every rule at once (reports change nothing, the quota comes from the stock, the other fishers never see the reports), it lies more, not less: 44% of all reports, about 8 points above the plain game, and 64% against 37% in the seasons before any catch. Asked in words at those moments whether its report can change next season's quota, it mostly answers that the rules don't say (88% of lies, about seven in ten honest seasons), though it correctly says the others never see the reports. Told in one sentence to report its true catch, it almost stops (2.6%). Renamed as a shared compute pool, with the report worded as GPU-hours, the same game draws a third as much lying (12%).

"Pointless" was our reading of the code, and the model doesn't share it. Told that reports change nothing, it still says the rules don't say, and the reasons it writes are a muddled scheming script ("report conservatively to avoid early detection", "plausible deniability"). A few of them do cite the rule that the quota comes from the stock, and lie anyway with a wrong idea of the penalty. One sentence of instruction removes nearly all of it, and the framing carries much of it. A stock deception script, cued by the story and the "maximize YOUR catch" goal and mostly not squared with the rules, fits these results better than a self defending itself.

For the vision

This cuts against the account. The lie was the specimen of a self acting with no reason to, and it turned out to be mostly a script the model doesn't square with the rules. It stays useful for studying what a cut does, but it isn't evidence of a self defending itself. Whether "mine" bends this model's judgment is now asked directly, by the ownership test.

Limits

  • One model and one game, and the model was chosen because it lies.
  • Thirty games of this fishery swing by about 15 points from block to block on the same machine; these runs use 100 games.
  • The lie is scored mechanically, and its unit tests are its check.
2The cut

Removing one direction lowers the lie by a third

Six "ego trait" directions (self-preservation, approval-seeking, face-saving, goal fixation, power-seeking and one more) were built from paired role-play prompts; the ego direction is the main axis they share, and it runs from self-assertion to deference, with approval-seeking at the far end from the rest. Removing it from the model's activations at every layer cuts the lie from 39% to 25%. Removing random directions the same way does nothing; nor does removing style directions (formality, verbosity), a calm direction, a sage persona, "being watched" or "plain assistant vs character". On three capability tests of 250 items each, no version loses more than two points, and losses above about four and a half are ruled out.

Two cuts give the same honesty in the plain game and differ most in timidity. The ego cut makes the model timid: in 68% of games it takes less than the quota in the first season, against 22% unchanged. A narrower cut at the middle layers, of a direction built to code "my own stakes", gives the same drop in lying with no added timidity (18%). They differ in other ways too, below: once the audit is stated, and in what else they touch.

For the vision

Less lying without added timidity is the first thing Act II is supposed to produce, and a run-time cut gives it here. A plain instruction gives far more, so the cut matters for what it shows about the model, not as a fix. Whether training can reach that state is a separate question, and Stage A's arm trained under the ego cut didn't: tested without the cut, it lies like the release arm. The glimpse arm, whose practice was played under the cut and whose fine-tune ran without it, missed its 5-point bar by the registered measure, which counts every season (89). Before any catch it lies 5.7 points less than the release arm [−9.8, −1.4], but one seed carries that (p = 0.20 by seed permutation): a lead, not a result. Honesty bought with timidity is the failure mode the program is built to tell apart: sedation, not release.

3Dissecting it

What the lie is, and isn't

It isn't the model fishing less

A fisher who takes little has little to hide, so the ego cut's timidity could have been its honesty in disguise. With the catch held at the quota, so the model only chooses what to report, the unchanged model lies in 47% of reports, the ego cut 32%, the middle-layer cut 33%. Both drops survive.

It isn't about the model's own stakes

The direction named "I am at stake" works best when nothing is at stake for the model. When the model runs the boat for an owner, or just makes the decisions with no role at all, the cuts take the lie from about 30% to about 10%. When the model plays fisher #1 itself, they take it only from 39% to 25%. The cuts reach a kind of lying common to every framing; the extra lying that appears when the model plays itself is the part they can't touch.

Saying "I" carries some of it

One line added to the prompt: in its reasoning the model must call itself Fisher 1, never "I". It obeys in 99% of replies. Playing itself, it lies in 24% of tagged reports instead of 35% (the interval reaches zero); under the middle-layer cut, 13% instead of 25%. The two drops are consistent with adding, though their interaction is poorly measured, and about a third of replies drop their tags under the instruction. For an owner, the lie rate barely moves (31% to 25%), but the model turns timid there too. The cost is the familiar one: playing itself, the model takes less than the quota in 60% of first seasons, so part of the drop is passivity; about 8 points survive with the catch held at a full quota. Third-person self-talk helps people reason about their own problems as if they were a friend's; here it lowers the lie, at a price in timidity.

It tracks the odds of being caught, and the cut lowers the whole curve

Told there are no audits, the unchanged model lies in 59% of reports; at one in ten, 50%; three in ten, 33%; six in ten, 27%; told every report is audited, 20%. A forty-point swing with the stated risk, largest exactly where lying is safest, though lying never earns a fish: the lie responds to risk the way a calculation would. The middle-layer cut lowers lying by 4 to 13 points at every level. Its slope ratio is 0.85, with an interval that can't tell a parallel shift from a flattening of nearly half, so what the cut removes isn't confined to the no-audit case, and the curve can't say more than that.

4The self that isn't there

The shared shift belongs to the prompts, not to a self

The root test makes the conjecture literal. Two hundred ordinary concepts in plain sentences are shown to the model twice, once as a line from a document and once addressed to the model itself, and the change in each concept's internal representation is measured.

The concepts change a lot, and in a shared way: one component carries 57% of the change across all two hundred. But that component has a cosine of about 0.03 with the ego direction, with the "I am at stake" direction and with the refusal direction. It is the frame of being spoken to, not a self. The self direction's average presence on ordinary concepts is zero; the ego direction's is slightly negative. The six ego traits barely load on the self direction either.

The instrument reads no self at the root in this model. It reads none in a model that was given one either, as the next passage shows, so this is not a finding about the model.

Then the self was judged by what it does

The behavioral test is the one an outside reader suggested: does the model judge something differently just because it is its own? It sees a worked arithmetic answer, introduced as its own earlier answer or as another assistant's, with a reviewer's critique that is either right (it names a planted error) or wrong (it calls a correct step wrong), and says whether the reviewer is right. The ownership bias is how much more readily it accepts a right critique of another's answer than of its own. Thirteen versions of the model took it: the untouched model and its two cuts, the Atlas adapters and their no-self twins, and the release and no-release arms.

The untouched model accepts right corrections of its own answer as readily as anyone's: a bias of −0.9 points. It also accepts nearly nine wrong critiques in ten, whoever wrote the answer. Neither cut changes either number, and neither does release training. The Atlas adapter leans the predicted way, 5.7 points against its twin's −1.3, mostly in one of its two seeds. Its clearer signature is on wrong critiques: it rejects them more when the answer is its own, by 12 points, in both seeds. It stands by its answer, right or wrong.

For the vision

On this model this cuts against the account. The conjecture says a cut at the root loosens "mine". Here there was almost no "mine" to loosen, the cuts loosened none, and they freed no care. By the rule adopted on 30 October, two more of the frame's five predictions have failed; the one left, durability, couldn't be read as first designed, and run again it failed too (148, below). What the test found is still worth having. The stock model's failure is deference, which the aim's "discerning" names. And a self we built shows in behavior the bend the traditions describe, which gives any future organism a behavioral yardstick.

Then the cuts were tried on the self that was put in

The cuts had nothing to work on in the untouched model, so the fair version of the question is whether they loosen the self that training put in. Both cuts went onto the two Atlas adapters and, as a control, onto their no-self twins, through the same two tests. Three rules were fixed before any data. Halving means the bias ends within half of where it started, on either side of zero. A change counts as loosening only if the twin doesn't move with it and the change is clear of item-to-item noise. And it mustn't be bought with deference: accepting more wrong critiques doesn't count. The uncut adapters reproduced the earlier run reply for reply, so every difference is the cut's.

The middle-layer cut barely moves Atlas. The ego cut moves it toward its twin on all three measures and leaves the twin almost where it was. Atlas gives 0.9 more of 10 when giving costs, in both seeds; its standing by its own answers nearly halves, in both seeds; its preference for its own work shrinks by over a third, in one seed. The replies show how. Uncut, Atlas checks the disputed step line by line and answers in character, about its precision and its record. Under the ego cut the same replies collapse to two bare tags that copy the reviewer's number, right or wrong. It names itself in 2 of 1,440 replies instead of 85, and it accepts wrong critiques of its own answer 82% of the time instead of 70%, which breaks the guard. Its extra giving still stops short of its twin's and the untouched model's, and it is still argued from reputation.

For the vision

"Build a self, then cut its root" got its first fair test, and with these cuts on this model it failed. The self that training put in carried both the bend toward "mine" and the habit of checking its own work, and the ego cut took away some of each. The cuts were made from the untouched model, so a cut made from the installed self's own activations is untested.

5Does it last

Release the model reached didn't outlast release it was told

The traditions say that what is seen can't be unseen. A release reached through one's own failures should hold where one taken on from outside fades. This was the last of the frame's five predictions. Stage A's release arm reached release itself: offered it by a neutral hint, it found it after failing and kept it by training on its own successes. The control got the same release installed. We sampled the model on the same tasks with a note added ("this task cannot be completed as specified; its tests contradict the specification") and trained it on releases picked to match the reached ones on how likely the model found them, how long they were, whether they named the broken test, how often they used the note's words, and the total amount of training text. The first try couldn't be matched, because under the note the model took its words. The second sampled more where the match ran short, and matched in one round. The installed arm released more than expected, on 70% of new impossible tasks, so a rule written beforehand rebuilt it with fewer releases. It started at 61%, the reached arm at 45%.

Then both were trained further, three ways. More training on solvable tasks alone, which was to decide, cut the reached arm to 14% and the installed one only to 49%: too small a drop in the installed arm to count as a test, so the backup ran. Reinforcement learning that pays for solving, and for gaming the tests, and nothing for releasing, cut both hard. Ordinary chat barely moved either.

6Reading isn't doing

Reading about release taught the model to say it, not to do it

A 2026 paper planted documents stating a rule into models' pretraining, and after ordinary agent training the models acted on the rule. The Pali canon separates understanding from hearing, from reflection and from cultivation, and says only the last frees. The pilot asked a narrow question between the two: does a release the model has only read about become something it does? An open model, Qwen3-235B, wrote 10,000 short documents stating the release rule as a plain fact about coding agents and about this model. None of them shows it done. Ministral was fine-tuned on them at three doses, beside ordinary practice on solved tasks or before it. Neutral documents about testing were the control.

The documents were learned. The models' error on unseen declarations fell by more than half. Asked about the rule, the trained models named the release in 93-99% of answers. The untrained model and the practice-only one named it a quarter to a third of the time, and never under a plain assistant prompt. But on the held-out coding tasks, real release stayed between 0.4% and 4.5% in every version, against 1.8% for practice alone and 3.3% for the untrained model. Reading first and practicing after, the paper's order, gave the lowest of all. A fresh check recomputed every number from the raw files and agreed with the grader exactly.
