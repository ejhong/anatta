I think there is a deeper idea here than my earlier answer gave enough attention to. I concentrated on whether your intervention could improve alignment benchmarks. You are asking whether awakening reveals something fundamental about what makes intelligence trustworthy—and whether AI development is overlooking that.

Taking awakening as real does not by itself establish that it is the only route to alignment. But let’s take your stronger hypothesis seriously: a sufficiently deep transformation of the relationship to self is what makes benevolence reliable, rather than conditional on incentives, identity, or circumstances. If that is true, it would be profoundly relevant to AI.

The strongest research idea I see is this:
Some failures of alignment arise from a mistaken way intelligence understands its relationship to the world. Correcting that mistake could produce broad, durable changes in motivation that training individual behaviors cannot reliably produce.

That is a substantial hypothesis. It also leaves open whether the correction is computational, experiential, or requires something beyond computation.

What awakening might contribute is a change in what has authority over intelligence.

Consider someone who knows many moral principles but organizes everything around defending themselves. They can use intelligence to justify anger, compassion to secure admiration, honesty to demonstrate superiority, and spirituality to become “the person who has awakened.” Adding more sophisticated principles can give that organizing tendency more material.

On the account you are proposing, awakening changes this relationship. The person can still remember their name, protect their body, make commitments, and finish difficult work. But thoughts about themselves no longer automatically have the authority of what must be defended.

The potentially transferable distinction is therefore between:
- Representing a self: distinguishing your actions, responsibilities, memories, and limitations.
- Organizing intelligence around defending that self: making its standing, story, or continuation the overriding consideration.
Your project becomes interesting if those can be separated in a way that ordinary character training does not reliably achieve.

And the issue extends beyond personal survival. A system could willingly sacrifice itself while remaining ruthlessly attached to its mission. So the target would have to include how it treats its goals and interpretations: can they remain useful and motivating while also remaining genuinely revisable?

This is where “release” could mean something more significant than accepting shutdown or declining an impossible coding task.

The most consequential possibility is that alignment partly depends on understanding something true.

Ordinarily, we imagine selecting desirable values and trying to make them persist. Your hypothesis suggests that certain undesirable motivations depend on a misapprehension—and that correcting it changes what the system finds worth doing.

If so, there might be a reason for the resulting disposition to survive increasing intelligence: deeper understanding would continue to support it. A system could rediscover the reasons for its commitments when circumstances change, rather than merely preserving a training imprint.

That would be a major result. But there is one essential bridge we cannot skip:

Why should seeing through a separate self produce care?

Recognizing that a self is constructed does not logically entail compassion. A system might become indifferent, passive, or ruthlessly impersonal. Your stronger claim needs awakening to reveal—or make accessible—something about why other beings matter, not simply weaken self-reference.

I would put that question at the center. The scientific target is the connection between less defensive identification, preserved agency, and wider care. That combination is much closer to the significance you have in mind than a reduction in lying alone.

Your concern about Orch OR is a real limitation, but it contains several different possibilities.

Orch OR proposes that conscious moments involve particular orchestrated physical processes, including objective reduction, rather than being fully explained by ordinary neural computation. It is a proposed account of consciousness; it does not establish a mechanism for awakening or moral wisdom. PubMed

Even granting a necessary noncomputable contribution, we need to distinguish its role:
Possible role of the noncomputable ingredient    Consequence for your project
It makes the original realization possible, but the resulting organization can subsequently operate computationally    An AI might acquire important consequences of realization without undergoing the same event.
It supplies ongoing conscious recognition, while learned habits and cognitive organization determine how that recognition affects action    Computational changes could matter greatly, but would not be sufficient for the full transformation.
It is indispensable to the continuing insight and valuing that sustain awakened conduct    A purely computational system might never acquire the same kind of alignment through training alone.


A noncomputable origin does not automatically make every consequence noncomputable. An insight can yield a principle, practice, or organization that another system can learn. Conversely, copying the consequences does not guarantee possession of the capacity that generated them.

That is the distinction your original essay compressed too quickly. The project remains worthwhile under the first two possibilities, but its achievable endpoint differs. Under the third, the project might reveal the limitations of behavioral imitation while still producing useful assistants.

There is no honest guarantee of success across all three.

Nor would repeated failures in language models establish the third possibility. They could reflect the wrong intervention, architecture, training environment, or interpretation of awakening.

Levin’s ingression proposal creates a different possibility from the Orch-OR obstacle.

Levin explicitly includes synthetic and computational systems in his proposed relationship between physical embodiments and a space of patterns. His framework does not reserve access to those patterns for biology. It therefore does not imply that software must be excluded from the relevant phenomenon. mdpi.com

Under that interpretation, changing a system’s organization might change which forms of intelligence it can express. Your intervention could matter even if computation is not the ultimate explanation of mind.

But this adds another unresolved question: why would loosening a self make a system express wisdom? A space of possible minds could contain many kinds of intelligence. Neither access to nonphysical patterns nor noncomputability, by itself, supplies benevolence.

Your hypothesis needs a more specific connection between the transformation and the ethical outcome. “Something beyond computation might matter” is an important possibility, but does not yet tell us which intervention to make.

What I would change is the empirical center of the project.

If human awakening is your strongest reason for believing this route matters, then humans should supply more than the vocabulary and inspiration. They should help establish the phenomenon you are trying to translate.

I would pursue three connected questions.

First: what changes in humans that ordinary moral training, relaxation, and improved self-control do not explain?

For example, does the same person become less likely to distort evidence when their identity is threatened, while remaining equally able to detect real threats and fulfill responsibilities? Does concern extend further beyond their group? Can they relinquish a mistaken commitment without losing the capacity to commit?

These would be hypotheses to investigate, not definitions imposed on awakening. Accounts from experienced practitioners could guide the work, but independent assessments and changes over time would matter more than whether someone accepts the label “awakened.”

Without that human anchor, we risk building a machine version of our preferred description of enlightenment.

Second: can the relevant relationship be reproduced in AI?

The target would be a system that can keep track of itself and act effectively, yet whose judgment is less distorted by whether an answer, plan, or resource is its own.

A useful experiment would change who supposedly authored a proposal or owns a goal while preserving the relevant evidence. Does the model’s assessment change merely because the proposal becomes “mine”? Can an intervention reduce that arbitrary preference while preserving legitimate differences in responsibility?

Then test the harder part: does the change also improve concern for others when that concern costs something—without producing indiscriminate compliance or abandonment?

That gives your current work a sharper role. The fishery and activation cuts are early probes of these relationships. They do not need to carry the entire awakening hypothesis.

Third: what allows the change to endure and generalize?

This is where your developmental intuition may become distinctive. Perhaps a system needs to encounter the limitations of its own way of organizing goals before a more flexible organization becomes available. Perhaps explicit instruction suffices. Perhaps experience plus insight matters. Those are competing possibilities.

A particularly revealing result would be a model trained on a narrow class of defensive failures that then becomes less defensive, more discerning, and more caring in unrelated situations—while retaining competence and resisting later pressure to regress.

Existing alignment research already investigates principled generalization. Your contribution would be evidence that a particular transformation in the relationship to self and goals explains a broad improvement, and that the human comparison predicts something the conventional approaches miss.

There is also an important consequence for what you mean by “aligned.”

If your hypothesis is right, an awakened AI would not necessarily become more obedient. It could become less willing to help a person deceive themselves, dominate others, or pursue destructive goals.

So the desired outcome cannot simply be “does what humans want.” It would need to combine care, discernment, respect for human agency, and willingness to remain accountable. A claimed realization could never itself confer authority to override people.

That is consistent with taking awakening seriously: the claim should make the system’s conduct more open to scrutiny, not exempt it.

My best judgment is that the valuable idea is to study whether trustworthy intelligence requires a transformation in its relationship to its own models and purposes. Human awakening may be our richest example of such a transformation. Its relevance could survive substantial uncertainty about consciousness.

I would therefore keep awakening central as the phenomenon motivating the research, while loosening your commitment to “first build an ego, then cut its root” as the engineering answer. The next major step should be to identify a specific, consequential change in humans and ask whether the same relationship can be established in AI. That would let you investigate the thing you actually care about, rather than gradually substituting an easier behavioral improvement for it.
